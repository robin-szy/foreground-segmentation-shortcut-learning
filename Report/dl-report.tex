\begin{abstract}
This project will focus on image classification using a deep learning model trained on a 10-class
subset of the iNaturalist-12k dataset available on Kaggle, provided by Aryan Pandey. The dataset
contains 12.000 images across biological categories such as Amphibia, Mammalia, and Plantae. The
classes are fairly balanced with 1200 available images for each of the classes.
The main goal is to design, train, and evaluate a convolutional neural network from scratch on
the aforementioned dataset. As a possible extension, the project may explore feature engineering
techniques such as extraction of the biological object (animal or plant) to study the impact of
background information on classification. Additional extensions include the creation of a second
model by transfer learning and the comparison of the latter with the model created from scratch.
\end{abstract}

Deep learning models achieve strong performance on standard benchmarks, yet their limitations remain an active area of research. Geirhos et al.\ define shortcuts as decision rules that perform well on benchmark datasets but fail under more challenging or out-of-distribution conditions \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}. Such shortcut learning is particularly problematic in safety-critical applications such as autonomous driving, where models must generalize to unseen environments \cite{Beery_2018_RecogTerra}. At the same time, prior work has shown that image backgrounds can contain highly predictive information and may substantially contribute to classification accuracy \cite{xiao2021noise}.

This project investigates whether reducing background information through automatic object segmentation can improve robustness and reduce shortcut learning in biological image classification. A 10-class subset of the iNaturalist-12K dataset from Kaggle was used \cite{inaturalist12k}. The dataset is well suited for this task because it contains substantial variability in habitats, lighting conditions, and objects. A segmentation pipeline based on Segment Anything (SAM) \cite{sam_1,sam_2} and CLIP \cite{CLIP} was developed to automatically extract the foreground object without requiring manual annotations. The resulting segmented masks were used to crop images to the region of interest and to train CNN-based classification models. The performance of models trained with this segmentation-based regularization approach was then compared against equivalent models trained on the original, non-segmented images.

Shortcut learning has emerged as an important limitation of deep neural networks \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}. In ecological datasets, background information and hence the location can become a strong shortcut because images from the same location often share very similar environmental context, particularly in datasets collected from fixed animal camera traps \cite{Beery_2018_RecogTerra}. Geirhos et al.\ argue that shortcut learning should not only be studied through in-distribution accuracy, but rather by manipulating the evidence available to the model, such as background, texture, or color, and observing how predictions change \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}. Several studies demonstrate that image backgrounds alone can contain sufficient information for classification \cite{xiao2021noise, obj_recog_without_background}. Xiao et al.\ showed that models trained on ImageNet often strongly depend on contextual background information, while Rosenfeld et al.\ demonstrated that even small background perturbations such as noise can significantly alter predictions \cite{xiao2021noise, rosenfeld2018elephantroom}. Geirhos et al.\ further found that CNNs frequently rely more on texture cues than on object shape, which may contribute to such behavior and provides additional motivation for segmentation-based approaches that emphasize object structure over contextual texture information \cite{geirhos2018TextureFeats}.

These findings motivate approaches that explicitly separate foreground objects from their surrounding context. However, many supervised segmentation methods, including segmentation-based variants of YOLO, require manually annotated masks and labels for training, which is highly labor-intensive and not given for the iNaturalist dataset \cite{YOLO}. Such annotations are not available for the iNaturalist-12K dataset used in this project. However, Segment Anything (SAM) \cite{sam_1, sam_2} provides a way to automatically generate segmentation proposals without requiring manual annotation. To semantically identify relevant masks, this project additionally uses CLIP \cite{CLIP}, which uses text-image similarity to semantically score and label images. The resulting pipeline is conceptually related to copy-paste augmentation methods \cite{copy_paste_augmentation}, where segmented foreground objects are inserted into alternative backgrounds to improve robustness and reduce contextual bias.

\subsection{Results}

The quality of the segmentation masks was manually evaluated on a sample of 200 masks, with 20 masks per class. A mask was counted as good if it captured the main foreground object without substantial missing regions or excessive background. The results in table \ref{tab:mask-quality} show significant variation between classes. In particular, masks were weak for \textit{Animalia}, but very reliable for \textit{Aves}, \textit{Mammalia}, and \textit{Fungi}. Therefore, the evaluation will additionally inspect whether classes with higher-quality masks also benefit more from segmentation in terms of macro-accuracy and AUC.
